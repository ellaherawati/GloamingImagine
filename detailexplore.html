Learning to Read
the Road

By Marcus Steinfeld April 12, 2026
Scroll
Home › Explore › Learning to Read the Road

I remember the first time I noticed it. We were somewhere in Andalusia, three days into a week-long trip that had turned wetter and steeper than the route profile suggested. My riding partner, a former criterium racer from Ghent, kept drifting to the inside of every bend with what looked like inexplicable ease. I was losing a second per corner, braking too early, carrying too much worry into the apex. There's a language the road speaks — in gradient, in surface, in shadow. Most riders hear it as noise. The few who listen find something else entirely: a fluency that changes how you move through the world. "You're reading the wrong part of the road," she said at the next café stop, without looking up from her espresso. It was the kind of advice that sounds simple until you realise it contains everything. Tarmac is not a neutral surface. Its texture, its colour, the way it gathers moisture or repels it — these are all signals available to anyone paying attention. Darker patches ahead can mean shade and damp. A fine-grained surface grips differently than the coarse chip you might find on descent sections resurfaced in a hurry. The faint sheen of condensation at 7am is not the same as rain slick. The primary technical adjustment is gaze. New riders look too close — a habit inherited from walking, where the immediate ground matters most. On a bike at speed, the relevant horizon shifts dramatically. You need your eyes perhaps 30 to 50 metres forward, sometimes more on fast descents. The near ground takes care of itself through feel; the far ground is where decisions happen. This connects to something psychologists call anticipatory processing — the brain's ability to build forward models of an environment from incomplete information. Elite cyclists do this across multiple timescales simultaneously: the immediate reaction, the tactical mid-ride decision, and the broader route management running as a background thread.

Learning to Look Further Ahead

"You're not driving to where you are. You're driving to where you're about to be. Most people never make that shift."

— Tomas Velt, pro cycling coach, Girona

The test of road-reading is not in the predictable. It's in the moment when something violates your expectation — a patch of gravel carried onto tarmac from a field entrance, a shadow concealing a frost heave, a corner that tightens more than the approach suggested. These are the moments that sort fluency from mimicry.

The Flemish riders who grew up on cobbled bergs developed something particular: a tolerance for chaos, an ability to process unpredictable surfaces as normal rather than alarming. You can see it in their relaxed upper bodies even on rough ground — tension is the enemy of feel, and they learned early to stay loose.

For those who didn't grow up on cobbles, the skill is acquirable — but it requires deliberate attention, not just accumulated kilometres. Ride familiar roads at a reduced pace and force your gaze further forward than feels natural. Notice what arrives in your peripheral awareness. After a while, push the pace back up and try to retain the same gaze distance. The sensation of flow, when it comes, is unmistakable.

Back in Andalusia, by the fourth day, something had shifted. I wasn't consciously applying technique. The road was simply making more sense — its intention readable before it arrived. My partner noticed without comment, which felt like the highest possible form of acknowledgement.

That's the curious thing about fluency of any kind. The moment it becomes conscious effort again, something has already gone wrong. The road reads best when you stop trying to read it, and simply let it speak.
